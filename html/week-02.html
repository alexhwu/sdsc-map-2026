The Software Crisis and the 1968 NATO Conference

Where the phrase 'software engineering' came from, and why it was coined as an aspiration rather than a description. Students read primary-source transcripts from Garmisch 1968 and discover that nearly every complaint in the room is still live today.

20 minutes instruction  •  25 minutes workshop

By the end of today you can…

  1. Explain what the 'software crisis' was in concrete terms: cost overruns, schedule slips, and unreliability at a scale hardware engineering had never produced.
  2. Identify at least three complaints from 1968 that are unchanged in 2026, and one that genuinely got solved.

Two ways this goes

The same phase of the lifecycle, done well and done badly — both from real projects

When the process holds

Version control as a solved problem

Configuration management was a top-three complaint in 1968 - teams genuinely lost track of which source produced which binary. Distributed version control solved it so thoroughly that modern developers cannot imagine the problem.

When it doesn’t

Estimation, 58 years running

Every major estimation technique proposed since 1968 - function points, COCOMO, story points, T-shirt sizes - has failed to produce reliably accurate schedules. The field has largely moved to managing the consequences of bad estimates rather than fixing estimation.

AI in the loop

You will use AI this week. Here is where it earns its place — and where it lies to you.

Use it for

Use AI to do historical cross-referencing you couldn't do by hand - but verify every claim against the primary PDF.

Try this prompt

"Here is a passage from the 1968 NATO software engineering report. Identify the underlying engineering problem in modern terminology, then name the specific modern practice that addresses it. Flag any part of your answer you're not confident about."

Do not trust

Models confidently misattribute famous quotes to this conference. Dijkstra's 'go to' letter (1968) and the 'software crisis' phrase get scrambled together constantly. Check the PDF with Ctrl-F before you write anything down.

Homework — The Complaint Ledger, Extended

Estimated time: 70-90 minutes

  1. Find TWO more complaints in the Garmisch PDF that we didn't cover in class. Quote them briefly (under 15 words each) with a page number.
  2. For each, write 150 words: is it solved, managed, or still true? Name evidence.
  3. Pick the one you think is genuinely permanent. Defend it in your journal in one paragraph.
  4. AI task: ask an assistant to argue AGAINST your 'permanent' claim. Record its strongest point and whether it changed your mind.
