Slide 03 of 8Core concept

The six phases of every lifecycle

Every project passes through all six — whether or not anyone names them

  1. ConceiveDecide there is a problem worth solving, who it is for, and whether it is worth building at all.
  2. SpecifyPin down what “done” means: requirements, constraints, and acceptance criteria you can check against.
  3. DesignChoose the structure — components, interfaces, and data — and name the trade-offs you are accepting.
  4. BuildTurn the design into working code in small, reviewable, version-controlled steps.
  5. VerifyProve it does what the spec says: tests, review, and checks in the real target environment.
  6. Operate / RetireRun it, monitor it, fix it — and eventually shut it down on purpose instead of letting it rot.
Waterfall, Scrum, Kanban, and XP are arrangements of these same six phases — not alternatives to them. Skip a phase and it doesn’t disappear; it just happens implicitly, usually at 2am.
Slide 04 of 8Case study

Two ways this goes

The same phase of the lifecycle, done well and done badly — both from real projects

When the process holds

SQLite's test suite

SQLite ships with roughly 590 times more test code than library code and has three independent test harnesses. It is one of the most deployed pieces of software on Earth. That ratio is a lifecycle decision, not a coding decision.

When it doesn’t

The 'it works on my machine' startup

Countless teams have shipped a demo that worked, then discovered the demo was the only configuration that ever worked. No specification of the target environment means no way to verify against it.

Slide 05 of 8AI practice

AI in the loop

You will use AI this week. Here is where it earns its place — and where it lies to you.

Use it for

Use an AI assistant as an interviewer, not an author. Have it interrogate your process description until the gaps show.

Try this prompt

"I'm going to describe how I built a project. Ask me one question at a time to find the steps I did implicitly or skipped entirely. Don't summarize until I say stop."

Do not trust

The model will happily invent a flattering, well-organized narrative of your process if you let it write instead of ask. If its summary sounds more professional than your actual project felt, it made something up.

Slide 06 of 8Workshop · 25 min

Reverse-Engineer Your Own Process

  1. On your own: write down what actually happened between ‘I had the idea’ and ‘it worked’ on your most recent project. No editing for flattery.
  2. In pairs: swap accounts and map your partner’s story onto the six phases. Which ones were skipped, compressed, or done at 2am?
  3. As a class: we tally which phase gets skipped most — then you work out what skipping it actually cost you.
  4. Sign the AI contract in your journal: every AI-assisted submission says what the AI did and what you checked.

Slide 08 of 8Homework

Homework — Journal Entry 1 + Process Autopsy

Estimated time: 60-75 minutes

  1. Set up your Engineering Journal (a repo, a Notion page, or a physical notebook - it must be timestamped and append-only in spirit).
  2. Write a 400-600 word autopsy of a past project: what you built, what went wrong, and which of the six phases you skipped.
  3. Read the SQLite testing page. Write one paragraph on which claim in it you find hardest to believe, and why.
  4. AI task: run the interviewer prompt above. Paste the transcript into your journal and highlight ONE thing the AI surfaced that you had not noticed - or write 'nothing new' if that's the truth.
